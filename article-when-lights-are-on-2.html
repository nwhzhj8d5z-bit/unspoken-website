When Lights Are On, But Nobody's Home

By Farida Younes · 5 min read

They say, “When life gives you lemons, you make lemonade.” But they never mention what happens when life gives you cactus… What are you supposed to do?

Are you supposed to squeeze them as well? Or simply accept the fact that life isn't giving you a drink, not even a chance to make one?

Perhaps that's what nobody tells you about growing up: sometimes, life doesn't give you lemons, and no matter how hard you try, there's no lemonade waiting at the end of the process.

Since I was young, I had one dream. Just one. A dream that kept me alive, gave me hope, and motivated me whenever I was down or on the verge of giving up.

We grew together, that dream and I. I always thought we were inseparable.

That dream made me a very special person. It created a passionate and fearless girl, someone willing to give her all just to make that one dream a reality.

But life had a different opinion. It separated us in the most brutal way possible, and ever since, the lights have been on, but nobody's home.

For the very first time, I feel useless. I feel like a guest in my own life rather than its main character. I no longer feel like I'm living; I'm simply surviving through the situations life (and my father) keep putting me in.

I started losing all my special traits: my smile, my energy, my thoughts, my passion.

I felt as if I were aging.

My mother said, “This is adult life.” She said that as long as I remained attached to that dream, I would never be able to accept my reality.

But how do I tell her that detaching myself from that dream means detaching myself from who I am? That it means I will no longer be the same person?

I can't seem to find the perfect word to describe how I feel now. Am I disappointed? Lifeless? Desperate?

Or am I simply… a young adult?

Perhaps that's the strangest part of this stage of life. You're not quite a child anymore, but you're not entirely an adult either. You're stuck somewhere in between, expected to understand the world while still trying to understand yourself.

Someone stuck between two contradictory statements: “You're old enough to make certain decisions and take full responsibility for them,” and “You're still young; you don't know what's best for you.”

At this age, we become pawns in our parents' hands. Someone might argue that we've always been.

Yeah, but we were never aware of it.

It's our emotional awareness, our consciousness of our own desires, that makes us realise just how little control we sometimes have over our lives.

And isn't that ironic? We spend our childhood waiting to become adults, convinced that growing up means freedom. We imagine being able to make our own decisions, choose our own paths, fall in love with whoever we want, pursue whatever career we dream of, and finally live life on our own terms.

Yet, when we actually get there, we discover that freedom comes with a price tag.

Suddenly, every decision has consequences. Every dream needs a financial plan. Every career choice has to be justified by its stability.

We are told to follow our passions, but not at the expense of financial security. To be independent, but not too independent. To take risks, but to make sure we have a backup plan. To enjoy our youth, but also to start building our future before it's too late.

We are expected to have our lives figured out at an age when most of us are still figuring out who we are. We are supposed to choose a career that will define the next forty years of our lives, make decisions that will determine our financial future, maintain relationships, build connections, and somehow find time to enjoy being young.

The problem is that nobody teaches us how to deal with the possibility that our lives might not follow the timeline we imagined. Nobody prepares us for delayed dreams, unexpected failures, missed opportunities, or the uncomfortable realisation that sometimes, working hard isn't enough.

And perhaps that's what makes young adulthood so exhausting. It's not necessarily the amount of responsibility we have, but the uncertainty that comes with it.

We're old enough to understand what we might lose, but young enough to still have so much to lose.

We're growing up in a generation where the possibilities seem endless, yet the opportunities to actually pursue them often feel limited. We're constantly encouraged to become the best versions of ourselves, while simultaneously being reminded of everything we cannot afford, cannot risk, or cannot control.

“We're old enough to understand what we might lose, but young enough to still have so much to lose.”

We are told that we can be anything we want, but rarely taught what to do when we discover that wanting something isn't always enough.

And because, as Aristotle once suggested, the proper function of man is to live, not merely to exist, I am beginning to question something at this particular stage of my life.

What does it actually mean to live?

Is it simply having a stable career, paying your bills, fulfilling your responsibilities, and making decisions that guarantee a comfortable future? Or is there something more to it?

Something that cannot be measured by a salary, a degree, a job title, or the approval of the people around us?

When did having a life become so different from actually living one?

And perhaps the most frightening question of all: what happens when the person you become is someone you never wanted to be?

Are we really in control of our own lives? Or are we simply determined by circumstances beyond our reach? Even when we dream, fight, and try, is nothing really going to change in the end?

Are we choosing our paths, or are we simply choosing between the paths that life has made available to us?

Will I continue to survive as a guest in my own life?

Is this what life reveals to us when we grow up? Is this what the future we once dreamed of has promised us?

Maybe growing up isn't about losing our dreams. Maybe it's about discovering how difficult it is to hold on to them when reality keeps asking us to let go.

And maybe that's the real tragedy of young adulthood: not knowing whether we should keep fighting for the lives we once imagined or learn to love the lives we never planned to have.

Determinism?
